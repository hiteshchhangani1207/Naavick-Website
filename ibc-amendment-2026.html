IBC Amendment Act, 2026
A Clause-by-Clause Analysis

The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (Amendment) Act, 2026 — Act No. 6 of 2026 — is the most comprehensive reform to India's insolvency framework since the Code's enactment in 2016. Spanning 72 clauses, it recalibrates the admission process, codifies the clean-slate doctrine, reverses Rainbow Papers, overrules Vidarbha, and introduces four wholly new regimes. Compiled by Naavick Legal.

Overview

The Amendment at a Glance

The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (Amendment) Act, 2026 — Bill No. 107-F of 2025, passed by Parliament and published in the Gazette of India, Extraordinary, Part II, Section 1 on 6 April 2026 — contains 72 clauses amending the core corporate-insolvency architecture (Part II), the personal-insolvency architecture (Part III), the regulator's powers (Parts IV and V), and inserting four wholly new regimes.

It comes into force on such date as the Central Government may appoint by notification, with different dates permissible for different provisions. Several substantive provisions carry express transitional Explanations addressing pending CIRPs and liquidations.

Key Numbers

72 clauses · 4 new regimes (CLRP, Group Insolvency, Electronic Portal, Cross-Border) · 2 sections decriminalised (ss. 74 & 76) · 5 sections omitted (ss. 38–42, 55–58) · Analysis current as of the 2026 amendment cycle.

Part I — Admission & Process

Admission, Withdrawal & Moratorium

Section 7(5) — Mandatory admission; Vidarbha overruled Clause 4

The word "may" in s. 7(5) — which the Supreme Court read in Vidarbha Industries Power Ltd v. Axis Bank (2022) as conferring discretion to refuse admission even on proven default — is replaced by "shall." The Adjudicating Authority must now, within 14 days, either (a) admit if default is established, the application is complete, and no disciplinary proceeding is pending against the proposed IRP; or (b) reject with a prior 7-day cure notice. A 7-day cure notice must precede rejection. If no order is passed in 14 days, the AA must record reasons in writing.

Explanation I closes the gate entirely: once clause (a) is satisfied, "no other ground shall be considered to reject." Explanation II makes an Information Utility-recorded default sufficient to ascertain default. The universe of admissible facts at the admission stage is now closed to three: (i) default, (ii) completeness, (iii) IRP disciplinary status.

Practical Impact

Corporate debtors lose the Vidarbha defence of solvency or viability. Financial creditors get near-certain admission on a clean IU record. The residual protection against abuse now sits in the new s. 65 / s. 64A civil penalties rather than at the admission stage.

Section 12A — RP-driven withdrawal; hard window Clause 8

Section 12A is substituted entirely. The key changes: withdrawal is now on an application by the Resolution Professional (not the original applicant), with 90% CoC approval. A non-obstante bar forecloses withdrawal (a) before constitution of the CoC, and (b) after the first invitation for resolution plans. The AA must decide within 30 days or record reasons.

This legislatively displaces the flexible inherent-power approach of Swiss Ribbons v. Union of India (2019) and Brilliant Alloys v. Rashmi Metaliks on timing, and overrides NCLAT rulings allowing post-invitation or pre-CoC withdrawals. Pair with new s. 33(1A)/(1B): the Act narrows exit by settlement but widens rescue by CIRP restoration.

Section 14(3)(b) — Moratorium shields CD from guarantor's recovery Clause 9

A new Explanation to s. 14(3)(b) provides that the s. 14(1) moratorium "shall also apply where the surety seeks to initiate or continue any action or proceedings against the corporate debtor pursuant to a contract of guarantee." This is a different vector from SBI v. V. Ramakrishnan (2018): creditors may still pursue guarantors (the main limb of s. 14(3)(b) is intact), but a guarantor who has paid cannot enforce subrogation or indemnity against the CD during CIRP. Read with the new s. 31 Explanation II (post-plan extinguishment of subrogation), this plugs the circular-claim loop.

Part II — Resolution Plan

Clean Slate Codified & Dissenting Creditor Floor

Section 30(2)(ba) — Dissenting financial creditor minimum; Amit Metallics settled Clause 18

A new clause (ba) is inserted: a resolution plan must pay dissenting financial creditors not less than the lower of — (i) their liquidation value under s. 53; or (ii) the amount they would receive if the plan's distributable amount were distributed in the s. 53 waterfall priority order. The floor is now codified and formula-driven, ending the post-Essar Steel controversy where the Amit Metallics / DBS Bank v. Ruchi Soya line of cases left the minimum subject to CoC commercial wisdom.

Section 30(4) is also amended to require the CoC to record reasons for plan approval — a transparency upgrade making CoC decisions more susceptible to judicial review.

Section 31 — Statutory clean slate; licence continuity; retrospective reach Clause 19

This is the single most consequential clause for deal certainty. The changes include:

New s. 31(6): On plan approval, all pre-approval claims against the CD and its assets stand extinguished — including tax assessments and statutory demands. No proceedings may continue or be instituted.

New s. 31(5): Government/regulatory licences, permits, registrations, quotas, concessions and clearances associated with the plan shall not be suspended or terminated during their remaining term if the plan obligations are met.

Explanation III: Sub-sections (5) and (6) apply retrospectively from the commencement of the Code, except matters that have attained finality.

Explanation I: Does not affect claims against promoters, guarantors, or joint/several obligors. Explanation II: A co-obligor who pays post-approval loses its right of indemnity/subrogation against the CD.

Case Law Impact

Codifies Ghanashyam Mishra & Sons v. Edelweiss ARC (2021) and Essar Steel. Overrides the Rainbow Papers-style revival of Government dues at the plan stage. The retrospective Explanation III is the principal litigation flashpoint going forward.

Additionally, the CCI/anti-trust approval is now required before submission to the AA (not before CoC approval), and the AA now has a 30-day clock for plan approval with a cure-opportunity before rejection.

Part III — Liquidation

CoC Continuity, Timelines & Waterfall Reform

Section 33(1A)/(1B) — One-time CIRP restoration before liquidation Clause 20

Before ordering liquidation on grounds of no plan (ground a) or plan rejection (ground b), the AA must now consider a CoC application (≥66% vote) to restore the CIRP — giving up to 120 additional days to complete it. Restoration is available only once. The AA must pass any liquidation order within 30 days of intimation or record reasons. Additionally, s. 33(1)(b) now includes a new moratorium on the CD's assets during liquidation, and the CoC may resolve to dissolve the CD directly (feeding into s. 54(2A)).

Sections 34 & 34A — Mandatory separate liquidator; CoC chooses and can replace Clauses 21–22

The RP of the CIRP shall not be appointed as liquidator of that CD (new s. 34(4)). On a liquidation order, the AA refers to IBBI for recommendation, and IBBI proposes a different IP within 10 days. New s. 34A gives the CoC (≥66%) the right to replace the liquidator at any time with another consenting IP — mirroring CoC control of the CIRP stage and ending the automatic RP-continues-as-liquidator practice.

Sections 21(11) & 35(2) — CoC (not SCC) supervises liquidation Clauses 13 & 23

New s. 21(11): where liquidation is initiated, the CoC continues and supervises the liquidator. The Stakeholders' Consultation Committee (SCC) — whose advice was merely advisory — is effectively superseded. New s. 35(2): the CoC supervises the conduct of liquidation in the manner specified by the Board.

New s. 35(1)(a): the liquidator maintains an updated list of RP-verified claims — no fresh invitation to creditors. The RP's claims verification during CIRP now becomes decisive for liquidation as well.

Sections 38–42 omitted — Liquidation claims machinery dismantled Clause 25

All five sections governing the separate liquidation claims process — invitation, verification, admission/rejection and the s. 42 appeal — are deleted entirely for post-commencement liquidations. Claims verified by the RP carry over; the liquidator updates rather than re-adjudicates. A transitional carve-out preserves the old process for liquidations initiated before commencement.

Section 52 — 14-day election; 66% consent for shared security Clause 31

A secured creditor electing to realise outside the estate must inform the liquidator and identify the asset within 14 days of liquidation commencement, failing which the security is deemed relinquished to the estate. Where an asset is charged to more than one secured creditor, realisation requires consent of creditors holding ≥66% by value of claims secured by that asset. CIRP/liquidation costs and workmen's dues must be deducted from realisation proceeds and transferred to the liquidator.

Section 53 — Waterfall recalibrated; Rainbow Papers reversed Clauses 2, 32, 57

This is a three-pronged legislative reversal of State Tax Officer v. Rainbow Papers Ltd (2022):

s. 3(31) Explanation: "Security interest" excludes interests created merely by operation of law — statutory charges are not security interests.

s. 53(1)(e)(i) Explanation: Government dues — whether or not secured by a statutory charge — for the 2 years preceding liquidation are distributed under priority (e) (below workmen/secured/employees/financial-unsecured), with the remainder under (f).

s. 53(1)(b)(ii) Explanation: A secured creditor is secured only to the extent of the value of the security interest relinquished; the shortfall ranks as unsecured.

s. 53(2) Illustrations settle the inter-se subordination debate: contractual subordination of workmen to secured creditors is disregarded; subordination between two secured creditors is valid.

Key Takeaway for Government & Tax Authorities

Government and tax authorities lose their "secured creditor" status. They drop to priority (e)/(f) in the waterfall — well below financial creditors. The s. 3(31) exclusion of statutory charges is the doctrinal keystone of this reversal.

Section 54 — 180 + 90 day hard timeline; CoC manages avoidance post-dissolution Clause 33

The liquidator must completely liquidate and apply for dissolution within 180 days, extendable by the AA up to 90 days for sufficient reasons. Where avoidance/fraudulent-trading or s. 47 proceedings are pending at the time of dissolution, the CoC determines how they are pursued and how proceeds are distributed — these proceedings survive dissolution. Direct dissolution (without completing full liquidation) is enabled on a CoC decision under s. 33(2).

Part IV — Avoidance & Recovery

Wider Look-Back, Creditor Standing & Guarantor Assets

Sections 43/46/50 — Look-back anchored to initiation date Clauses 26, 27, 30

The avoidance look-back window for preferential (s. 43), undervalued (s. 46) and extortionate (s. 50) transactions now runs from the initiation date (i.e., filing) rather than the insolvency commencement date (admission). This adds the filing-to-admission gap back into the window, neutralising the value-leakage caused by tribunal backlog and admission delays. New umbrella definitions — "avoidance transaction" (s. 5(2A)) and "fraudulent or wrongful trading" (s. 5(9A)) — are inserted to enable clean cross-referencing throughout the Act.

Section 47 & Section 26 — Creditors may pursue all avoidance categories; proceedings survive completion Clauses 16, 24, 28

Old s. 47 gave creditors standing only for undervalued transactions where the RP/liquidator failed to act. New s. 47 extends creditor standing to all four avoidance categories — preferential (s. 43), undervalued (s. 45), extortionate (s. 50), and fraudulent/wrongful trading (s. 66). Where the RP/liquidator has not reported a matter, a creditor (alone or jointly), member or partner may apply directly to the AA. The AA must direct IBBI to initiate disciplinary proceedings against a defaulting RP/liquidator.

New s. 26: filing or completion of CIRP/liquidation does not affect avoidance/fraud/s. 47 proceedings — they continue post-completion. This closes the "abatement on completion" gap and overrides the Venus Recruiters line of cases.

Section 28A — Guarantor's enforced assets integrated into CD's resolution Clause 17

New s. 28A enables a creditor who has taken possession of a personal or corporate guarantor's asset (e.g., under SARFAESI) to permit transfer of that asset as part of the CD's resolution plan, with CoC approval. Layered approval gates apply depending on whether the guarantor is itself in CIRP/liquidation or personal insolvency. On transfer, full title vests in the transferee; proceeds are adjusted against the guarantor's debt, with surplus returning to the guarantor.

Part V — New Regimes

CLRP, Group Insolvency & New Frameworks

Chapter IV-A (ss. 58A–58K) — Creditor-Led Resolution Process (CLRP) Clause 40

A wholly new Chapter IV-A introduces the Creditor-Led Resolution Process — a faster, largely out-of-court, debtor-in-possession resolution channel for notified (smaller) corporate debtors initiated by a financial creditor. Key mechanics: the FC obtains 51% class approval → gives the CD 30 days to represent → re-confirms 51% → appoints an RP who makes a public announcement; the CD may object within 30 days; the process must complete in 150 + 45 days; the CD's Board of Directors retains management (debtor-in-possession) while the RP may attend and veto Board resolutions; a moratorium may be applied on RP application with CoC/51% approval; conversion to full CIRP is available on CoC 66% vote or failure; withdrawal requires 90% CoC; plan approval requires 66% CoC then AA sanction.

The CLRP is heavily dependent on Central Government notifications (thresholds, eligible class) and IBBI regulations. The Fast-Track CIRP (old Chapter IV, ss. 55–58) is simultaneously omitted.

Chapter VA (s. 59A) — Group Insolvency Clause 42

New Chapter VA provides a statutory rule-making framework for coordinating the insolvency of two or more connected corporate debtors — a long-awaited codification of the ad hoc approaches taken in Videocon and Srei. The Central Government may prescribe conditions for group insolvency including: a common Bench, coordination between CoCs/RPs/liquidators, a common insolvency professional, a group CoC, a binding coordination agreement, and cost treatment. "Group" is defined by control or ≥26% voting rights under the Companies Act, 2013 framework.

Section 240B — Integrated Electronic Portal Clause 71

New s. 240B mandates the establishment of an integrated electronic portal for all IBC processes — filings, notifications, public announcements, creditor claims, plan submissions and related communications. This operationalises the long-stated goal of end-to-end digital insolvency administration.

Section 240C — Cross-Border Insolvency Framework Clause 71

New s. 240C provides a legislative hook for the Central Government to prescribe cross-border insolvency rules — the enabling framework for what will likely be a modified-UNCITRAL Model Law adoption. This remains dependent on delegated legislation.

New Civil Penalty Architecture (ss. 64A, 67B, 67C, 183A, 235A) Clauses 44, 48, 58, 68

A calibrated civil penalty framework replaces the criminalisation of routine breaches:

s. 64A / s. 183A: ₹1 lakh to ₹2 crore for frivolous/vexatious proceedings under Parts II and III respectively.

s. 67B: ₹1 lakh–₹2 crore for breach of the s. 14 moratorium; ₹1 lakh–₹1 crore or 20% of plan distribution (whichever higher) for breach of an approved plan.

s. 67C: ₹1 lakh–₹2 crore for an operational creditor who conceals a pre-existing dispute or payment in a s. 9 application.

s. 235A (substituted): General contravention penalty ≥₹1 lakh per day, up to 3× loss/unlawful gain, capped at ₹5 crore where unquantifiable.

Sections 74 & 76 omitted — Decriminalisation Clauses 49, 50

Criminal liability for breach of moratorium/approved plan (s. 74) and false PPIRP information (s. 76) is removed. The conduct is now addressed by the civil penalty architecture above. A saving clause (s. 235A Explanation II) preserves pending prosecutions and imposed punishments under the old provisions.

Part VI — Personal Insolvency

Personal Guarantors & Bankruptcy Reform

Sections 96 & 124 — No interim moratorium for personal guarantors to CDs Clauses 51, 55

New ss. 96(4) and 124(4): the interim moratorium provisions shall not apply where the application is for a personal guarantor to a corporate debtor. This removes the automatic pre-admission stay that personal guarantors obtained merely by filing, which had been used tactically to stall creditor recovery. The scheme upheld in Dilip B. Jiwrajka v. Union of India (2024) remains valid in structure, but the interim moratorium for this class is legislatively excised.

Section 164A — Transactions defrauding creditors in personal bankruptcy Clause 56

New s. 164A inserts the personal bankruptcy analogue of s. 49 (corporate insolvency). Where a debtor entered an undervalued transaction deliberately to put assets beyond a claimant's reach or to prejudice a claimant, the AA shall restore the position as if the transaction had not occurred. Good-faith, for-value, without-notice third-party protections are built in. This closes the asset-shielding loophole in personal bankruptcy and extends the corporate anti-avoidance principle to individuals and guarantors.

IBBI Regulations Updated in Tandem

CIRP Regulations — Notification IBBI/2026-27/GN/REG152 dated 01.06.2026 (w.e.f. 02.06.2026) (master alignment), with earlier 2025–26 notifications REG126, REG127, REG128, REG130, REG133, REG135 and REG141/REG153.

Conclusion

A Restructured Framework for a Maturing Market

The IBC Amendment Act, 2026 is unmistakably legislative in its intent: it resolves the major fault lines exposed by a decade of IBC jurisprudence with surgical precision. Vidarbha's discretionary admission gateway is closed; Rainbow Papers' elevation of Government dues is reversed; the clean-slate doctrine is hardened and given retrospective reach; avoidance actions survive process completion; and an entirely new creditor-led track is created for smaller defaults.

The most significant open questions will concern the retrospective effect of the s. 31 Explanation III (claims extinguished even in pre-2026 cases not attaining finality), the operationalisation of the CLRP threshold notifications, and the development of Group Insolvency rules under s. 59A.

For practitioners, insolvency professionals, resolution applicants and financial institutions, the 2026 amendments demand immediate review of pending matters, ongoing CIRP strategies, liquidation timelines and plan structures — the changes apply to pending proceedings in significant respects.
